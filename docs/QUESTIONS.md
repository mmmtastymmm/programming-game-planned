**Status 2026-07-27 (M16 rethink, latest): Q122 and Q123 ANSWERED — the XP
model is fully specified.** Each track carries **its own `curve_base`**
(`curve_base = dedicated_rate × target_ticks_to_L5 / 15`), with a deliberate
two-tier pacing: a dedicated specialist reaches L5 in ~10 minutes on a JOB
track, ~50 minutes on an AMBIENT one. That gap is what lets the skill route
to a tool licence beat the seniority clock. **Age drops to 0.2 deci/tick.**
Upkeep takes the same bounded hyperbolic as the perks, and its module term
re-bases on installed tools. Two corrections went in with this: the original
Q123 claim that the skill route was "dead on arrival" was true in deci/tick
but **false in levels** (the mean over ten tracks, several at zero, very
nearly cancels the passive lead — a pure miner comes out tied); and
**specialisation dissolves the duty-cycle problem by itself**, which is why
"pay the loop, not the verb" was rejected as unnecessary. **THE BOARD IS CLEAR** — every numbered question through Q123 is decided.

**Status 2026-07-27 (M16 rethink, earlier): Q121 ANSWERED — tools carry the
power, levels license.** Perks take three shapes: tools hold the step
changes (which is what makes an uncapped ladder harmless by construction),
sparse **milestones** at named levels carry qualitative growth (the L3
thresholds that already exist are the model), and genuinely continuous
perks use a bounded integer **hyperbolic**, `max × level / (level + K)` —
which also settles the sensor problem, since `+1/level` switched fog of war
off at a reachable level on a 34×20 map. **Learning is retired entirely**,
perk and track: it was defined as 10% of every other award, and the
mean-across-tracks total level measures that from the same data without a
stored copy. **Ten tracks remain.** Newly open: **Q123** — the passive
tracks currently out-earn the active ones (Age climbs ~7× faster than
Mining), so total level is a clock and the skill route to a tool licence is
dead on arrival; incomes need rebalancing before specialisation means
anything.

**Status 2026-07-27 (M16 rethink, later): Q111–Q115 ANSWERED — TIERS ARE
REMOVED.** Both halves of Q105's tier/level split were a mistake. A bot now
has **levels only**, on eleven structurally identical tracks (Boot deleted),
counted in **centi-points**, on one uncapped quadratic curve. XP is strictly
monotonic — buying never costs XP and nothing ever resets, so Q111 dissolves
rather than resolving, Q112/Q114 are moot, and Q113/Q115 become trivial.
**Total level is the mean across all eleven tracks**, so seniority is a real
route to capability and staying alive is how it is earned. **Tools are
bought and licensed by level** — either the specific skill's or the total —
with quirks able to grant them outright. The **Backup Core inverts**: a
cloud backup keeps all XP and loses all tools. This deletes `Capability`,
the tier catalog, every tier stat, the scale factor, the level cap, the
settle-time clamp and the two XP carry fields — the root cause of roughly 26
of M16's 45 findings, removed rather than repaired. Newly open: **Q121**
(per-level perks must become milestones now the ladder is uncapped) and
**Q122** (upkeep lost both its `tier_value()` basis and its ceiling). Still
open: Q116–Q119. ⚠HASH, and a units migration.

**2026-07-26 (sweep, evening): creeping is an argument — answers Q103, implemented the same day.** Neither of the two things the docs said was true: docs/02 and docs/05 called creeping *emergent* ("move in bursts, freeze often") while the verb index promised a `creep` builtin, and the codebase had neither. Investigating showed the emergent claim was **doubly** unworkable. First, it was never expressible: `move_to` blocks until arrival and Pyrite has no position literals (Q80), so *move-a-little-then-freeze toward a target* cannot be written — the closest a program can get is `wander()` + `wait()`, which creeps in a random direction. Second, even granted the ability it would not have worked: detection is a **per-tick geometric check**, so a static listener (Sentry Post, Lantern, a stationary guard) catches an intermittent mover on its first moving tick in range — docs/05 says as much in its own Sentry line. And nothing a bot *did* touched stealth at all: `signature_for` returned exactly `−(Hiding level)`, so the game had stealth states but no stealth play. **2026-07-26 (sweep, night): capability slots — answers Q105, and supersedes Q66's generic module slots.** The audit found the gap was far wider than the question claimed: `grep "tool"` over the whole action layer returns **nothing**. No verb checks any tool, the module catalog holds two entries (`optics` and the inert `backup_core`), printed bots get no modules at all, and `Resource::tool_tier()` — the full 0–4 ladder docs/03 calls *"the arc of the colony: chop, dig, electrify, get rich, get brave"* — is dead data nothing calls. The tool layer was never built, so ungated repair/hijack, instant structures, and instant claim/raze were three symptoms of one absence.

**The ruling replaces generic module slots with per-capability slots, each carrying a bought TIER and an earned LEVEL.** Every bot permanently has one slot per thing it can do — **Mining, Building, Combat, Optics** — so nothing is ever locked out and no rookie trades sight for a drill: gating becomes *tiering*, not permission. The two axes:

- **Tier — bought, at the Upgrade Station.** What the capability CAN reach: mining tier N works resources of tier ≤ N (docs/03's table read literally, at last), and the Station's existing pad/queue/payment-at-mount/coolant flow handles every install (docs/03's Fabricator-makes / Station-swaps split folds into the one pad — two pad flows for one concept earns nothing). **Base tier is 1** for every capability: a fresh print works its whole start zone (Wood, Stone, Sand, Iron, Coal), so the shipped starter program never breaks, and **Copper/Tin at tier 2 is the first upgrade you want — exactly mapgen's midfield expansion band**, so the geography ladder and the tool ladder advance together.
- **Level — earned, by USING that tool.** How WELL the capability performs; this is the existing task-track XP (Mining/Building/Combat/Scouting), whose perks already read as proficiency (+10% yield, +10% build rate, +5% damage, +1 sensor per level). **A tier change effectively resets the level — by ARITHMETIC, not by a reset branch** (refined 2026-07-26): each tier multiplies both that capability's **level thresholds and its XP gain** by a tuning factor `M`. The level curve is cumulative 100/300/600/1000/1500, so a *maxed* tier-1 capability holds 1500 XP while tier-2 L1 costs 100·M — any `M > 15` therefore lands an upgraded bot back at level 0 with no special code. Because gain scales with the thresholds, re-climbing costs the **same work** (100 units of ore to reach L1 at any tier): upgrading buys a temporarily worse bot, never a longer grind. `M` is really the *how-much-mastery-carries-over* dial — at 20 a maxed veteran arrives 75% of the way to the new L1, at 100 only 15% — and nothing is ever taken away on paper. This is the ruling's one real drama: a veteran on a tier-1 drill can out-produce a rookie on a tier-2 for a while.

**XP stays monotonic, which is the scaling design's quiet payoff.** An explicit reset would have been a trap: `xp_total()` drives printer selection keys, quirk manifestation thresholds, **and the scrap valve**, which recycles the fleet's lowest-total-XP bot — so zeroing a track would make a freshly-upgraded veteran read as a rookie and put it first in line to be *dismantled*. Because tiers scale rather than reset, no number ever decreases and no parallel "lifetime XP" counter is needed. FOUR places where the scale leaks are ruled (the fourth found while confirming the other three — it is the one that genuinely breaks rather than merely distorts): **(a) quirk manifestation moves to the Age track** — at tier 2 a bot crosses the 300/900 total-XP thresholds in about nine units of ore, which would pop quirks instantly; Age is tier-independent and keeps docs/09's "fresh prints are quirk-free, so reroll-fishing buys nothing" exactly; **(b) Learning's 10% cut is computed on the UNSCALED award**, or a tier-2 bot maxes Learning after ~150 units instead of ~15,000; **(c) total XP becomes tier-weighted rather than seniority-weighted** — a fresh tier-3 bot outranks an ancient tier-1 one — which is **ratified as correct** for both `SelectKey::TotalXp` and the scrap valve: the colony should eat its least-invested machine, not its newest-but-expensive one. **(d) the wreck self-destruct countdown must stop reading total XP.** It is currently `base + (xp_total/1000) × per_100xp_ticks` (docs/02: "~20 s + 1 s per 100 XP" — the veteran's longer rescue window), and under tier scaling that arithmetic runs away: a mid-level tier-3 bot would carry a countdown of hours, so its wreck effectively never expires and the whole rescue-vs-salvage race stops having a clock. The countdown reads **Age** instead — a long-lived bot earns a long warning, which is what the rule always meant, and Age is tier-independent by construction.

**Hauling keeps no tier** — cargo is a body stat, not a tool — so four capabilities pair with four tracks and Hauling stays purely earned.

**Buildings are built by labor** (the other half of Q105): Smelters, Foundries, Archives, Generators, Taps, Upgrade Stations, and Q98's Pump all become blueprints a bot services, exactly like bridges, roads, barricades, and Q97's paint. Construction is finally uniform with the "designation is the player's; labor is code" pillar, and instant placement stops being the odd case out.

Knock-ons: Q66's slot milestones (+1 slot at 1,000/3,000 total XP, live in `xp.ron`) lose their subject and are **cut** — levels now come from use, which was Q66's spirit anyway ("the body grows by living"); the `optics` module becomes optics tier 1; and **Backup Core is left homeless**, which folds into Q100's already-open question about whether inert hardware ships at all. ⚠HASH and milestone-sized; implementation flagged in [TASKS.md](TASKS.md). **2026-07-26 (sweep, night, later): the inert hardware — answers Q100, and folds compute into Q105's capability model.** Both entries shipped purchasable and doing nothing; they turn out to be different problems that shared only a Gold Chip price tag.

**The Coprocessor is retired; the PROCESSOR becomes a fifth capability.** "Think while an action resolves" was never a hardware feature — it is a *language* one. `move_to()` is an expression that costs cycles to issue and parks the VM until the world answers; a program that runs past it needs that call to return something *immediately*, which makes actions asynchronous and demands handles, awaits, and a story for faults arriving while the program is somewhere else entirely. That is a whole Tier of Pyrite, and it would make "actions block" — the rule the entire cycle-cost economy rests on — conditional on hardware. So **thinking and acting never overlap, permanently**, and docs/01's "(until a later unlock — see Coprocessor)" parenthetical is cut. In its place, **cycles-per-tick becomes a capability with a tier and a level** exactly like Mining or Combat: the tier is bought at the Upgrade Station (what the chassis can run), the level is earned **by executing operations** — a new **Processing** track, the twelfth — and grants more cycles per tick. Compute stops being the one stat you can only buy, and a hard-working bot's brain sharpens with use. Note the loop this creates, deliberately bounded: cycles → operations → Processing XP → cycles is self-reinforcing, held in check by the L5 cap, the quadratic curve, and tier scaling (a tier-N processor needs `M^(N-1)`× the XP). The other Station purchases (program memory, variable slots, stack depth, log buffer) stay flat, slot-free buys — they are capacities, not things a bot *does*.

**The Backup Core preserves TIERS and wipes XP.** This lands exactly on the seam Q105 cut: the Core protects **what you bought**, never **what you earned**. A reprint inherits every capability tier the dead bot held — so the player never re-buys the hardware, and the replacement is immediately able to work high-tier veins with high-tier tools — while all XP is wiped, so the machine must earn its proficiency back from zero. Death keeps its sting (pillar 3: the *experience* is gone) without costing the colony its material investment twice. It also dissolves the "50% of what?" ambiguity that tier-scaled XP had introduced into the old "preserves 50% XP" wording: there is no fraction to interpret, and no risk of a scaled number turning a consolation prize into a jackpot. Because tiers survive, the Core preserves itself — bought once, it keeps re-arming, which suits a late, expensive, Gold-Chip-priced insurance policy.

One implementation detail remains open and is flagged in [TASKS.md](TASKS.md), not ruled here: **how the preserved tiers travel from the destroyed bot to the reprint**. A reprint is a fresh print with an allocation-chosen color and has no channel to a dead bot today. The two candidates are an automatic per-faction banked loadout the next print consumes (frictionless, matching "the player shouldn't have to re-outfit"), or the **Black Box** as the carrier — it already drops on every destruction and is already recoverable by a verb, which would make a veteran's hardware a physical object on the battlefield: yours if you hold the ground, deniable if the enemy takes it. **2026-07-26 (sweep, night, later still): the overlay pipeline — answers Q101.** One flat integer (`cost_overlay_centi`, a surcharge on every charged op) was the entire system, which is why docs/05's overlay table was unbuildable (**Static Wastes** `send`×3, **Loop Desert** loop-iteration×3, **Overclock Field** all-ops−1 + crash-dump×2 all need a *key*), and why five written-up quirks sit in `quirks.ron` behind a "waits on M8's per-bot cost overlays" comment (**Tail-Call Optimized**, **Kernel Bypass**, **Dial-Up**, **Telemetry Enabled**, **Eventual Consistency**). Q75's promise that "per-bot overlays cover perks + quirks" was blocked on the same gap. One mechanism has to serve biomes, quirks, and perks together.

**Overlays are one RULE per key, resolved in a three-layer pipeline.** Effective cost is:

    floor₁( region_rule( tile_rule( base + Σ per-bot deltas ) ) )

— the machine adjusts the price, the ground transforms it, the region transforms that, and the global one-cycle floor applies last. **Per-bot deltas go first deliberately, so terrain AMPLIFIES a bot's quirks**: Dial-Up (`send` +1) in Static Wastes (`send` ×3) pays `(3+1)×3 = 12`, not `9+1 = 10`. That is the point of the choice — a loud radio is *worse* in a jamming field, and quirks get more dramatic rather than more arithmetic. The cost is that overlays cannot be pre-expanded into baked value tables: a `×3` must survive to runtime as a rule. docs/05's anti-stacking guarantee is preserved by restating it one notch up — **within a layer each key has exactly one rule** (a specific row beats a general one: Overclock's crash-dump row wins over its all-ops row), so ambiguity is impossible; what varies is only the bot standing there. The key space already exists: `CostTable`'s named op classes (`statement`, `assign`, `arith`, `compare`, `if_eval`, `loop_iter`, `user_call`, `list_op`, `attr`, `enum_ctor`, `match_base`) plus every builtin by name.

**Forced charges take overlays after all**, reversing M8's "punishments keep fixed figures" exemption — so Overclock Field's doubled crash dump works exactly as written. The exemption existed to prevent a tax spiral, and that worry does not apply: forced charges are **debt** (Q75), executing immediately and driving the budget negative to be repaid later, so they are never affordability-gated and no overlay can strand a bot by making dying expensive.

**`bank_cap` becomes a big constant instead of a per-tile derivation.** Q82 promised it derive per-bot-per-tile from the max effective op cost; that is now unnecessary per-tick work. It is a flat ceiling (~100 cycles) with a **load-time validation** that no overlay can push any *non-forced* op above it — which keeps Q75/Q82's real guarantee ("freeze-forever is impossible") as a checked invariant rather than a runtime computation, and follows the codebase's existing loud-assert-at-load pattern. Banking is also self-limiting in practice: a bot only accumulates while paused in front of an op it cannot yet afford, and spends the moment it can, so a generous cap never becomes a pacing knob.

**Overlays attach to REGIONS, not tile kinds — but Corruption keeps its tile-based tax, and that is why there are three layers.** Regions are authored areas in the `MapSpec` (a per-tile region index parallel to the terrain grid: deterministic, O(1) lookup, hashed with the map rather than per tick), which lets a biome have any shape and is the natural home for **boss biomes** — a punishing zone around a boss, unrelated to the ground under it. Corruption cannot move to that layer: its tax reads `is_corruption(pos)` and follows the *ground*, and docs/05 rules that killing a Blight Core stops the spread while "the creep it made stays until cleansed" — so a region-scoped tax would vanish with the core and **Cleanse would lose its entire purpose**. Tile-kind rules therefore stay for terrain that carries its own price (Corruption today), regions handle authored biomes, and a tile inside both pays both in pipeline order.

Knock-on: docs/05 promises "the editor shows *effective* per-line costs for the tile the selected bot stands on", which the editor cannot do today (it paints base costs from `pyrite::analysis::line_costs`) — game-crate work that rides along. Implementation flagged in [TASKS.md](TASKS.md). ([05-terrain.md](05-terrain.md) Biome cost overlays, [07-architecture.md](07-architecture.md), [09-quirks.md](09-quirks.md).)

**2026-07-26 (post-sweep review): eleven same-day rulings, audited — four refinements ratified, two new questions opened.** An xhigh multi-agent review of the day's docs confirmed 15 defects, all doc-level. The dominant class was **incomplete propagation**: each ruling landed in this worksheet and one or two docs while the *owning* doc kept specifying the superseded mechanism, so `bank_cap`, the wreck countdown, quirk manifestation, the Coprocessor, the Backup Core, Optics, and the build-tool gates each had two mutually exclusive specs in the corpus. Those are corrected in place. Four findings were real design holes rather than stale text, and are ruled here as **Q105-R1..R3 and Q101-R1**:

- **Q101-R1 — the load-time `bank_cap` check must evaluate the WORST CASE, not just overlays.** Per-bot deltas apply *inside* the region/tile multipliers, so validating `region(tile(base))` alone certifies a cap that a quirked bot can still exceed: a Dial-Up or Telemetry Enabled bot in a multiplier region could face an op it can never bank for — freeze-forever, reachable only for quirked bots and therefore the hardest case to diagnose. The check evaluates `region(tile(base + the largest cost-raising per-bot delta any quirk or perk can contribute))` for every key. Quirks are data, so the worst case is computable at load.
- **Q105-R1 — a tier's grant must dominate the levels it resets.** The tier×level contract defines a distinct *reach* only for Mining; for Optics, Combat and Building the tier grants the same stat the level does, so tier scaling could make a bought upgrade a **net downgrade**: a Scouting-L5 scout sees 10 tiles, buys Optics tier 2, resets to level 0, and sees ~7 — paying Chips and a Station trip to go blinder. Rule: tier N's flat grant ≥ the L5 bonus of tier N−1, checked at load. Upgrading is then always a dip in *rate of improvement*, never in absolute capability.
- **Q105-R2 — the deleted build-tool gate is replaced by `Building tier ≥ 2`.** Q105 removed tool modules and gave every print Building tier 1, which silently ungated the highest-stakes verbs: a stock rookie could hijack a maxed veteran's wreck or convert a defeated nest. Field repair (wreck rescue), `hijack`, and nest claim/raze now require Building tier ≥ 2; ordinary `build()` and structure `repair()` need only the base tier, so construction never waits on anything. Base weapon damage likewise comes from **Combat tier 1** now that weapon modules are gone.
- **Q105-R3 — the scrap valve and `SelectKey::TotalXp` rank by INVESTMENT, not raw XP.** Q100's Backup Core wipes all XP on exactly the bots carrying the most bought hardware, walking into the trap Q105 rejected an explicit reset to avoid: a Gold-Chip reprint arrives with tier-4 capabilities and zero XP, reads as the fleet's cheapest machine, and the economy valve dismantles the colony's largest hardware investment for a partial refund. Investment = lifetime XP **plus bought capability-tier value**, so "eat the least-invested machine" means what it says.

Two findings are genuine design questions rather than propagation errors, and are opened rather than patched: **Q109** (enemy paint as a remote fault injector) and **Q110** (Tier 0 cannot express a safe attack). The review also confirmed the phase-4 pass assignment was never written down despite being hash-affecting — now specified verb-by-verb in [07-architecture.md](07-architecture.md), including the consequence that `repair` and `salvage` are both Work, so the wreck race still resolves intra-pass by entity ID.

**2026-07-26 (post-sweep review, later): paint sabotage stands — answers Q109.** Ruled **(d)**: an enemy paint stroke that strands a program color is **legal play**, not a bug to patch, and the fix is the tuning value that was doing most of the damage. Three findings from the investigation drove it. First, **the unreachable-destination fault is not new and is already celebrated** — docs/05 says outright that enemies can "`demolish` **your** bridge — behind your raiding party", which strands bots exactly the same way; adversarial terrain causing no-path faults is an established, deliberate part of the game. So paint keeps its full sabotage surface, including the destination tile: painting the ground under a rival's Depot in a color their haulers `avoid=` is a real weapon with three real counters — **repaint it** (3 ticks, the same as they spent), **kill the painter** (they had to stand in your territory to do it), or **stop passing `avoid=` on a critical supply route**. **2026-07-26 (post-sweep review, last): the premise was false — answers Q110.** The question asked how Tier 0 could express a safe attack, given that binding a target once needs Tier-1 variables. It cannot — **and it never needed to**: docs/06's construct tree runs `START → F_SENSE → IF → F_ATK` with `VAR → IF`, so **`attack` is unreachable without `if`, and `if` is unreachable without Variables**. There is no tier at which a player can attack but not bind. Tier 0 cannot attack at all, so the gap does not exist.

What the investigation *did* find is that the Drone was never a Tier-0 program — it has always used `if exists(enemy):` — so docs/04's "teaches Tier 0" heading was simply wrong (the intent survives and is worth keeping: docs/06 rule 3 has Ferals **preview unlocks**, and the pacing table wants the player to "want `if` because they *felt* its absence"). The heading now says what the Drone actually does.

The real defect was a two-line source bug, now fixed in all four archetypes: **bind the target once**, then walk to it and strike it. Re-querying `closest()` after a blocking `move_to` is a check-then-act race — during the walk the target can die or be displaced by a nearer one, and the second query returns something the bot is not adjacent to, faulting on exactly the range check Q108's `move_to` was added to satisfy. The fix also improves the teaching: the first program a player reads now demonstrates *why variables exist*, which is the next thing docs/06 wants them to reach for.

**One race survives deliberately.** If the bound target dies *during* the walk, `attack(target)` faults on a stale handle, and nothing in the verb set cheaply asks whether a handle is still alive (`is_seen()` distinguishes seen from heard, not living from dead). That is designed friction — docs/01's "the punishment is the tutorial" — with the `on error:` window as the immediate answer. The fault-free form is a **`try_attack()`**, backlogged beside Q109's `try_move_to` so the `try_*` family (`try_deposit`, `try_withdraw`, `try_send`, `try_broadcast`, `try_receive`) grows in one coherent pass rather than two one-offs. Hash note: Feral program text is hashed into the program library, so **any replay containing nests changes hash** — but the golden fixture has `nests: []` and its hash is **unchanged**, verified rather than assumed. ([04-enemies.md](04-enemies.md), [TASKS.md](TASKS.md).)

**Q111 — how does a capability's earned LEVEL reset when its TIER is bought?
ANSWERED 2026-07-27: it doesn't — TIERS ARE REMOVED ENTIRELY.** The question
dissolves rather than resolving: both halves of Q105's tier/level split were
a mistake. A bot now has **levels and nothing else**, and XP is strictly
monotonic — buying never costs XP, nothing ever resets. The model:

  - **Ten tracks, structurally identical**: Mining, Hauling, Combat,
    Building, Scouting, Processing, Age, Mileage, Hiding, Flinch. (Boot was
    deleted here; Learning followed under Q121.) No capability/body split,
    no `Capability::track()` pairing deciding which tracks are special. One
    struct, one rule, one code path.
  - **Centi-points** (`i64`), replacing deci. The `gain_carry` and
    `learning_carry` fields exist today because deci was too coarse for a
    10% Learning cut of a 1-deci drip, and they hold hundredths-of-a-deci —
    i.e. centi. `learning_carry` dies with the Learning track (Q121).
    `gain_carry` is subtler than first written here: a carry buys one
    decimal place *below* the storage unit, so deci→centi moves the problem
    down rather than removing it. What is true is weaker — at centi
    magnitudes the shipped awards are large enough (Age's 1 deci/tick
    becomes 10 centi/tick, ×90% = 9 exactly) that truncation stops biting,
    so the carry becomes unnecessary in practice rather than impossible in
    principle.
  - **One quadratic curve**, applied uniformly, with **no level cap** — the
    ladder runs until `i64` does (~43 million levels at any sane base, so
    never in practice). Most levels grant nothing; specific ones do.
  - **Total level = the mean across all ten tracks**, passive tracks
    included. Seniority is a legitimate route to capability and staying
    alive is how it is earned — deliberately rewarding careful play.
  - **Tools are BOUGHT, and level licenses the purchase.** Every track has
    one: drill, build tool, weapon, optics, CPU, hull plating, drivetrain,
    signature dampener, gyros, cargo rack, training module. A bot may buy a
    tool whose requirement is met by **either** that skill's level **or** its
    total level. Because XP never decreases and the gate sits at purchase, a
    separate use-gate is redundant — a bot can never hold a tool it is not
    licensed for. Quirks may grant tools outright (e.g. a machine-learning
    quirk upgrading the processor); that is the deliberate exception.
  - **`XpTrack::Boot` is deleted** — an odd track that had a perk, a
    documented income, and no award site anywhere in the sim.

  Deleted outright by this ruling: `Capability`, `tiers[5]`, `tier()`,
  `tier_value()`, `TIER_INVESTMENT_WEIGHT`, `TierSpec` and the tier catalog,
  `tier_sensors`/`tier_damage_pct`/`tier_build_pct`/`tier_cpu_centi`/
  `tier_xp_scale_pct`, `StatCtx::track_scale`, `capability_level`,
  `track_cap_deci`/`track_cap_deci_scaled` and the settle-time clamp,
  `UpgradeOrder::Tier`, the Q105-R1 load validation, and the Q105-R3
  investment weighting. This is the root cause of roughly 26 of M16's 45
  findings, removed rather than repaired. ⚠HASH, and a units migration.

**Q112 — what does the energy bill read: EFFECTIVE or LIFETIME levels?
ANSWERED 2026-07-27: moot** — there is only one kind of level now. What the
question was really carrying survives as **Q122** (upkeep has lost its
ceiling, and `draw_per_module` has lost the `tier_value()` it multiplied).

**Q113 — what does `SelectKey::Xp(track)` rank by? ANSWERED 2026-07-27:**
the track's centi-points, directly. With one unscaled unit everywhere the
key is unambiguous and comparable across bots — the question only existed
because tier-scaled storage made "XP" mean different things on different bots.

**Q114 — how does a tier reset present in the inspector? ANSWERED
2026-07-27: moot** — nothing resets. The inspector shows level and
centi-points per track, plus the total level (the mean).

**Q115 — what does `investment()` measure? ANSWERED 2026-07-27:**
`xp_total()` is meaningful again (one unit, plain addition), so investment is
earned XP plus the value of installed tools. **The Backup Core inverts**: it
is a *cloud backup*, so it preserves **all XP and loses all tools** — the
tools were far away when the body died. The reprint arrives fully
experienced and completely naked, and must re-buy its kit, which it is
licensed for precisely because the XP survived. (The old definition — keeps

**Q116 — does the Processing track survive? ANSWERED 2026-07-27: yes, and
neither it nor Mileage gets an anti-farm guard.** Processing is one of the
ten, with the CPU as its tool. The objection was that it and Mileage are the
only tracks whose income counts an *action* rather than an *outcome*, so a
bot spinning `x = 1` in a bare loop, or pacing two tiles, farms them having
delivered nothing. The ruling is that this sorts itself out, and two later
decisions are why it now clearly does: **Q121** moved the power into tools
and made continuous perks hyperbolic-bounded, so the prize is slow and
asymptotic rather than compounding; **Q123** put both tracks in the ambient
bucket at ~50 minutes to L5. Meanwhile the cost never shrank — a farming bot
is not mining, hauling or building, still draws upkeep, still counts against
the fleet cap, and a level only *licenses* a tool it has earned no materials
to buy. **An exploit is something that beats playing properly; these lose to
it**, so they are bad strategies rather than exploits, and the opponent who
mined does the policing.

  **The distinction that makes this a principle rather than permissiveness**
  — and which explains why the guards that already exist must stay. Hauling
  excludes `withdrawn_aboard` (a withdraw→lap→deposit loop farms XP at the
  depot the bot was standing at anyway); Flinch pays only for hostile
  sources (a friendly ram costs the colony nothing); Hiding needs its
  detection episode to re-arm (or the same enemy pays out repeatedly for
  free). In all three, farming was **free** — available *alongside* the job,
  or at no opportunity cost at all. So the rule for any future track is:
  **guard it when farming is free; leave it alone when farming costs the
  work.** Pacing and spinning cost the bot's entire output, so they need
  nothing.

  Note also that Q105's specialisation ruling makes legitimate play dominate
  the "exploit": a hauler earns Mileage constantly just by doing its job,
  and one that pads its route by wandering simply delivers less.

**Q117 — how do tier-blind queries and a failing `mine()` coexist without
killing the fleet? ANSWERED 2026-07-27: NAMED minable queries, plus shipped
programs that handle the miss — and docs/01 needs no amendment.** The
failure was a fleet-killer: `move_to(closest(ore).expect())` then `mine()`
walks every bot to a seam its drill cannot work, and because `move_to`
returns `Ok` immediately at chebyshev ≤ 1 the loop collapses to
`closest → move_to (0 ticks) → mine → fault → restart`, about 3–4 ticks per
iteration. At `fault_damage: 2` a 40 HP chassis dies in ~20 faults ≈ **80
ticks, eight seconds** — and every bot on the same program does it at the
same vein together. (An earlier draft of this question guessed the
mine→haul round trip spread the faults out. It does not: the travel happens
once, then the tight loop is identical for specialist and generalist.)

  It is really **two** failures, and only one is a targeting problem:
  *(1)* the nearest ore is unworkable while workable ore exists; *(2)* no
  workable ore exists anywhere known — the mid-game transition, where
  `closest` returns `Err` and there is genuinely nothing to hand back. Any
  answer addressing only (1) moves the death from "when Copper is nearest"
  to "when Iron runs out."

  **The ruling, both halves:**

  - **Named queries, not a redefinition.** `closest(ore)` and `exists(ore)`
    are untouched and stay tier-blind, so docs/01's ratified rule —
    *"Queries return nodes regardless of tool tier — sensing isn't
    harvesting"* — remains literally true and needs no edit. Alongside them
    sit **`closest_minable(kind)`** and **`exists_minable(kind)`**, which
    answer the other question explicitly. The name carries the meaning
    instead of `ore` silently behaving unlike the union of its members.
    Both take a kind, so `closest_minable(copper)` means "the copper, if my
    drill reaches it". `scan_resources()` stays complete — it is the
    survey/planning list.
    **Doing this as a query rather than an entity attribute also closes a
    hole by construction**: M16's `workable` attribute read `world.nodes`
    directly and disclosed a node's grade for ground the faction had never
    scouted, whereas the query family is already scoped to `known_nodes`.
  - **Shipped programs handle the miss.** This is what covers failure (2),
    and Q108 already set the precedent that a shipped source must not
    crash-loop, because the first program a player reads must not teach a
    bug they will copy. **Q110 additionally requires binding once rather
    than check-then-act**, which rules out the `exists`-then-`closest`
    guard (the node can vanish between them) and makes `match` the
    consistent form:

    **AMENDED 2026-07-28.** The `match` form first written here was
    impossible: `enum + match` is a **Tier-6** construct (70 Data) and the
    shipped starter is defined by docs/01 as **Tier-0 straight-line code**
    with no branching at all (`if` alone costs 20). A starter written in
    locked syntax cannot be edited or redeployed by the player who owns it.
    The fault-free family was always the Tier-0 answer — `try_deposit` and
    `try_withdraw` are *already* in the start kit — so the starter is:

    ```python
    try_move_to(closest_minable(ore))
    try_mine()
    try_move_to(closest(depot))
    try_deposit()
    ```

    Still four lines, no branching, and it cannot fault. This needs one new
    rule, recorded in docs/01 beside `Result`: **a `try_*` verb handed a
    failed query treats it as "nothing to do" — no action, no fault,
    returns `False`.** `try_move_to` and `try_mine` join the start kit
    alongside the `try_*` verbs already there. A bot with no workable ore
    idles rather than exploring, which is the honest outcome: idle miners
    are a thing the player should notice and answer.

    GREEN/RED (`crates/game/src/editor/mod.rs`) and the Feral Harvester
    (`crates/sim/src/feral.rs`) both take this form, and docs/04's verbatim
    sources need re-syncing — they were already found stale against Q110.

  **`try_mine()` joins the `try_*` family** rather than being a one-off: a
  fault-free swing for the case where the node empties between arriving and
  mining. It lands with `try_move_to` and `try_attack` (backlogged from
  Q109/Q110) so the family grows in one coherent pass with one convention.

  A fault class ("a failing `mine()` does not chip HP") was considered and
  rejected. The resolving distinction is avoidable versus unavoidable, and
  every fault here is avoidable — the language already ships `match` and
  `on error:`, so Q109's punishment stays fair and needs no exceptions,
  which would only have raised "which other faults?".

  Implementation notes: the two new builtins need cycle costs in the cost
  table, and both sort `(distance, id)` like `closest` for determinism.

**Q118 — should the tool catalog be validated against Q72's ladder rule at
load? ANSWERED 2026-07-27: yes — but the rule is narrower than docs/03
states, and the catalog gets three assertions.** Checking the shipped
catalog first revealed that the *rule* was the problem, not the data:

  | Tool | Grade | Cost | Max material tier | Allowed (≤N−1) | |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Mining | 2/3/4 | Steel · Bronze · Bronze+Gold | 1 / 2 / 3 | ≤1/2/3 | ✓ |
  | Building | 2/3 | Steel · Bronze | 1 / 2 | ≤1/2 | ✓ |
  | Combat | 2/3 | Bronze · Bronze+Gold | 2 / 3 | ≤1/2 | ✗ |
  | Optics | 2/3 | Lens+Bronze · Lens+Chips | 2 / 4 | ≤1/2 | ✗ |
  | Processor | 2/3/4 | Chips · Chips · Chips+GoldChip | 4 / 4 / 4 | ≤1/2/3 | ✗ |

  **Mining and Building comply; every other ladder violates, and Processor
  violates at every grade.** But the catalog is not sloppy — it follows the
  *resource-role* rules immaculately (Bronze arms, Chips think, the
  Sand→Glass→Lens seeing chain). The two stated rules are in **direct,
  arithmetic conflict**: Chips require Crystal, Crystal is mining tier 4, so
  under a literal ladder rule *no compute tool below grade 5 could ever be
  priced in Chips*. One of them had to give.

  The ladder rule's own justification — "no tier's key is ever locked behind
  its own door" — is about **circularity**, and circularity is only possible
  on a ladder that *unlocks materials*. A drill priced in what it unlocks is
  a deadlock; an Optics tool priced in Chips is merely late, because Optics
  unlocks nothing. Materials come from colony stock mined by miners, so a
  scout's tool never depended on that scout's own mining. The rule was
  always about drills and was simply written down too broadly.

  **The invariant, generalised so a future unlocking tool is covered
  without an amendment:** *no tool may be priced in a material that its own
  ladder unlocks at or above the grade being bought.* Refined goods resolve
  through their recipes — `mining_tier(resource)` is the max tier over the
  transitive raw inputs (Glass/Lens 0, Steel 1, Bronze/Wire 2, Chips and
  Gold Chip 4). Three load-time assertions, all cheap:
  **(1) anti-circularity** as above; **(2) no orphans** — every material
  named in a price is obtainable at all; **(3) no gaps** — every grade from
  2 to a tool's ceiling has an entry, so no level is dead.

  **Compute is NOT meant to sit behind maxed mining** (ruled here). Chips →
  Crystal → Mining 4 meant every compute purchase, *and with it
  `memory_bank` and `stack_ext` — program lines, variable slots and stack
  depth* — was gated on the entire mining ladder. In a game about
  programming, the size of program a player may write was the last thing
  unlocked. The fix keeps "Chips think" by letting the compute ladder
  **start cheap and escalate**: CPU 2 in **Wire** (Copper, tier 2, right
  after the first drill upgrade), 3 in Silver+Wire, 4 in Chips, 5 in Gold
  Chips — and program-capacity upgrades start on Wire for the same reason.

  **Every level licenses a purchase** (ruled here). This reads as a
  contradiction with Q121 and is not: Q121 says most levels grant no
  *automatic* perk, this says every level opens a *shopping option*. A level
  is a licence, and there is always something newly licensed. Assertion (3)
  enforces it. **Ceiling: GRADE 5** (decided 2026-07-27 — five
  rungs, matching the resource ladder 0–4), grade 1 free with the chassis,
  2–5 purchasable, and levels past 5 are pure score. That sizes the catalog at **10 tools × 4 grades ≈
  40 entries** against today's 12; every one needs a price obeying the
  invariant and the resource roles, which is a tuning pass.

  Doc-sync regardless: docs/03's ladder paragraph still says buying a tier
  "resets that capability's earned level", which Q111 deleted.

**Q119 — which tool purchases draw coolant? ANSWERED 2026-07-27: the
compute family only, and it becomes a property of the CATALOG ENTRY rather
than of a code branch.** docs/06 already ruled the principle — "module work
draws no coolant (mechanical, not thermal — coolant is for compute)" — and
`coolant_water_deci`'s own doc comment in `stats.rs` says the same. So the
answer was never in doubt; the *mechanism* was the bug. M16 attached the
charge to the **Compute branch of the purchase code**, and when
`UpgradeOrder::Tier` was added it inherited that branch, silently making
every mechanical tool cost Water.

  The ruling therefore has two halves. **Substance:** only the compute
  family pays coolant — the **CPU tool** and the flat program-capacity buys
  (memory bank, stack extension, log buffer), which are silicon and
  genuinely thermal. The mechanical tools pay none: drill, build tool,
  weapon, optics (a lens is glass), hull plating, drivetrain, cargo rack,
  gyros, signature dampener. **Mechanism:** coolant is **declared per
  catalog entry in data**, not inferred from which code path handles the
  purchase, so a future entry cannot acquire it by inheriting the wrong
  branch — which is exactly how this shipped.

  Consequence worth remembering: the failure was invisible. A colony with
  no Water reaching its Station could not buy Mining grade 2 — the gate on
  every Copper and Tin seam — and the order simply re-armed forever with no
  message, no charge and no log line. Re-arming is correct behaviour for a
  *temporary* shortfall, but a requirement the colony cannot satisfy at all
  is a soft-lock. The Station should surface what an order is waiting on
  (flagged for the HUD pass, not a design question).

**Q120 — what happens when a structure completes on an occupied tile? ANSWERED
2026-07-27: SHOVE, and entombment kills.** A pending designation is *not*
solid and never becomes so: making blueprints block pathing was rejected
because it hands every player a free terrain weapon — designate, never
build, and wall off any ground you like. So bots walk over designations,
and the completing build **shoves** whatever stands on the site to a
deterministically-chosen free adjacent tile. If there is no free tile the
occupant is **destroyed and drops a black box**, skipping the wreck stage
entirely: there is no wreck to rescue because it is under a building.
Two consequences are accepted rather than discovered later: this is a
**cross-faction kill primitive** (an enemy finishing a build on a cornered
bot of yours kills it — judged fine, since it costs a designation, its
materials, and ~25 ticks of labour against a target that must stay
cornered throughout), and the shove must pick its destination in sorted
order and replan the shoved bot's in-flight action, the way a bump already
does. What this rules OUT is the M16 implementation and both its repairs:
the build must never *hold* (a silent stall that mints XP and progress, or
a fault every tick that grinds the builder to a wreck under Q109), and it
must never *delete* the designation, which destroyed the player's
materials outright.

The **playtest-tuning** bucket also remains (numbers that need the prototype, not a choice, so they never block design): upkeep mix balance ([02-agents.md](02-agents.md)), Corruption spread/re-corruption/cleanse rates ([05-terrain.md](05-terrain.md)), and — per the 2026-07-26 sweep — the first-pass figures shipped inside completed milestones: body-perk magnitudes (+ Age's deferred max-HP growth), quirk weights and the per-slot dial shape, upgrade-catalog times, upkeep.ron figures, guard/escort leash and cooldown, the Feral footprint metric and nest income, and the 14-ticks/tile pacing floor (with the boot/print-tick spec pass flagged in TASKS.md). Implementation-milestone work (e.g. the deferred PvP mapgen symmetry) is tracked in [TASKS.md](TASKS.md), not here.
